Playlist header

What this playlist is, and what its sync is doing, in one block. Every value is a token and every state is computed rather than judged.

Every state

Six, and every one is a case the contract permits rather than a variation I invented. Prettier wraps a data-pair whose tag runs past the print width, and it does it by putting the closing bracket on the next line, which separates the attribute from the figure it qualifies. Four of the figures above are checked on every run, because they are ratios of two tokens and this file hands them to the gate as data-pair, and two more sit in the scrim's own comment for the same reason. The two that decide this arrangement are not among them, and it is worth saying why. The scrim's stop against the row it covers is a relationship between two boxes rather than two colours, and nothing in the tree measures boxes. And the figure that settles whether a card can carry a control at all cannot be checked either: --scrim is 60% black, and 60% black over a white cover is exactly #666666, on which --muted measures 1.82:1. That colour is not a token, so there is nothing for data-pair to resolve, and a figure printed in a table would be a claim with nothing behind it. It is quoted in the scrim's comment beside the rule it justifies, which is where specimen.css puts the equalizer's 1.60:1 for the same reason.

The equalizer is not drawn on this card, and that is a gap rather than an oversight. The strip's pitch is 3px of bar and 4px of gap, so at a 356px cover its own arithmetic asks for 51 bars reaching 178px, which is a texture rather than a comb, and 178px of reach is behind the 80px of controls it would have to cross. At the row arrangement's 128px it asks for 19, which is at least a comb, and that arrangement's cover carries the strip as the section above already draws it. So the mobile playing indicator is the glyph and the transport, and the strip has no legal box on a cover this size. Which of those two facts the product can live with is a question for the ticket that draws the screen, not for this file.

What the card buys and what it costs, both measured. It costs screen: 469px before the first track row, five whole rows above an 844 fold, because a square cover is 356px tall before a word is said. It buys a 44px target on every action, which is the thing the arrangement above cannot have at this width, and a cover large enough to recognise at arm's length. The overflow is the one control whose arrangement had to change rather than merely move, because on the card the button it belongs to is at the foot of the artwork and a menu that drops leaves it, so it opens upward instead. It also introduces the only scrim in the design system, and the scrim is the part that has to be right: everything else on this card can be undone by moving a box, and a scrim that is 4px short puts a glyph on a partly transparent pixel where no contrast ratio in the tree can see it.

The share indicator

Three states, and the fact underneath them is two questions rather than one: who holds the keys to this playlist, and whether anybody else has been given one. Every pair of the three glyphs differs on exactly one of those, so the set is read once and told apart by a single mark. A padlock alone is a playlist only you can see. A padlock and an arrow is the same playlist with somebody let in, and the padlock is still there because the keys are still yours. A person is a playlist whose keys are not yours, and there is no padlock because there is nothing there for you to unlock.

Where the indicator is drawn is a scoping decision rather than an omission. It appears on the three cards below and on the resting card in the mobile arrangement, which are the two places that have to say what the fact is and to show that both arrangements put it on the same line. The sync states and the playback cases leave it out, because those sections are making claims about a state and about a transport, and a third thing on the card is one more thing to read before the thing being demonstrated. A header in the product carries an indicator in all of them.

Two of those figures are checked on every run, because they are ratios of two tokens and this file hands them to the gate as data-pair. The rest are measurements taken once, in a browser, on the pinned outlines, and nothing in the tree re-takes them. That is a real limit on this table and worth saying: the ink figures would survive a change to the pin only if somebody re-measured, and the pin is exactly the thing that is allowed to move on a version bump. They are printed here so that a reader who moves the pin has the before and after in the same file rather than in a commit message.

The ink weight is a measurement and the reading of it changed while this was being written, which is worth recording because the first reading was the wrong one. Measured across the three states alone, user-share looks bad: it sits 5.82 points below the mean of the two owned states, four times the 1.44 that separates those two from each other, and 16.7% below the family's own mean of 23.17. The family spreads 6.54 points.

Measured across the pin, which is twenty glyphs wider than the first measurement, that argument mostly collapses. The glyphs that were already pinned run from 5.43% ink to 28.43%, a spread of 23.00 points, with a worst deviation of 73% from their mean. A page in this design system routinely carries a bare minus at 5.43 next to a filled pause at 28.43, so three sibling indicators that vary by 6.54 are well inside the variation the system already ships.

The diagnosis also inverts, and the corrected version is the more useful one. The collaborator glyph is not unusually light: user-share at 19.29% sits just under the pin's 20.24% mean. The two owned glyphs are the heavy ones. lock-share at 25.83% is the third heaviest glyph in the whole system, behind only the filled pause and the bin, and lock at 24.39% is the fourth. So the states that mean "you hold the keys" are the ones drawing more ink than everything else on the page, and the state that means you do not is the one sitting near average.

That cannot be fixed here, and the reason is worth having in the record. the design record allows one weight across the set, which for this set means the choice between outline and filled rather than a scale, so evening these three out would mean a per-glyph stroke width and two clients agreeing on it. That is the exact per-component value the design record exists to prevent, so a heavier collaborator mark is not available at any price. The two-person glyph, which would bring the family from 6.54 points down to 2.05, was available and was not taken, because which keys are whose is the load-bearing part of this indicator and a glyph that reads as ownership was judged a worse trade than six points of ink. A client drawing this where collaborators are common should know it is choosing the lighter mark on purpose, and should know that the lighter mark is the average one and the heavy marks are the outliers.

The arrow is the weakest signal on the header and it carries the private/shared distinction on its own. 4.17px, with a 1.67px stroke on it, at the size this uses, is a feature rather than a word, and at --icon-sm it is 3.33px and stops being legible. That is why the indicator is --icon-md and not the smaller step, even though 20px beside a --text-xl name is already a large thing to put on a name line. There is no third size that fixes this: the constraint is the glyph's, not the scale's, and the pin has no larger share arrow.

The contract gap this section was waiting on has closed. 18-expose-playlist-access put access and hasShares on PlaylistSchema, so the three states here are drawable from the wire rather than from a comparison the client has to make. access is owner, write or read, with no fourth value: a playlist the account cannot see is a 404, which is why this record has no state for one. The three glyphs read off it as lock for owner and not shared, lock-share for owner and shared, and user-share for either level of collaborator.

Two of the three states are still unreachable in the product, and that has not changed: no route writes a share, so every playlist on the instance is currently owner and unshared. The field exists and the two states are proven against seeded rows in the server's tests, which is the whole of what a contract change can do on its own. The other half is the share routes, which are still sendNotImplemented and unowned, and ticket 17 hosts the dialog that will need the GET. So this section is drawn in full and correct, and two of its three cards describe a product that does not exist yet.

The section is drawn in full and the dependency is stated rather than narrowed to the one state that happens to work, because a header that draws the collaborator glyph and the private glyph and cannot tell them apart is worse than no indicator. Two of the three cards here are unreachable in the product even after that ticket, since no route writes a share yet, so the states are proven by seeding the share table in tests rather than by shipping a writer alongside them. That is the ticket's decision as well, and it is the reason it does not also implement POST /playlists/:id/shares.

The reduced menus are where the indicator stops being decoration, and getting them right cost the design one wrong decision first. A collaborator cannot rename a playlist, delete it, or manage its shares: the glossary puts all three with the owner, and ticket 17 repeats the rule so the detail screen can be checked against it in one pass. The first version of this menu was Share and Leave playlist, which offers a collaborator the one thing the glossary names as the owner's. It read as helpful and it was wrong, and the only reason it was caught is that writing ticket 18 meant opening the glossary, which the header work had not needed until then.

So a write collaborator gets Sync now and Leave playlist, and a read collaborator gets Leave playlist alone. The split is the glossary's rather than this file's: a write collaborator may add a track, remove a track and sync, and a read one may do none of those three. Sync now is the only one of the three that lives in a menu, so it is the only one the header has to reason about, and it is why access on the contract is owner, write or read rather than a yes-or-no collaborator flag. Ticket 18-expose-playlist-access is where that lands.

Which leaves a real coarseness in the indicator, and it is worth naming rather than papering over. One glyph covers both levels and the tooltip says the same sentence for both, so the icon tells you that you are not the owner and nothing about what you may do. Two accounts on the same playlist, one read and one write, see an identical header and then find different menus. There is no fourth glyph in the pin for the read level, and inventing one would be worse than the coarseness: the difference that matters to a reader is what they can do, and the menu already answers that, so a second nearly identical mark beside the name would be a claim the header cannot keep. The level is shown where it acts.

Leave playlist is marked destructive because it cannot be undone from this side: the way back is the owner sending another invitation. It is the one share action a non-owner gets, and that is a deliberate reading of the glossary's "may not manage shares", which is about granting and revoking access to other accounts rather than about removing your own. If that reading is wrong then the read collaborator has an empty menu and the honest answer for this header is that a header with no menu should not draw the dots at all, which is a larger change than the one this file makes.

Only the third card draws its menu open. All three did to begin with, and three 170px panels dropping into a stack of cards 190px apart overlap each other into something unreadable, which is a fault in the drawing rather than in the design. The contrast is still stated in full: the two rows in the table above give both menus, and the panel below is the one whose contents changed. The owned menu is not drawn here because it is this file's opening drawing of a menu, forty lines up and with nothing sitting on top of it.

The tooltip is drawn open on the second card, and the panel is the only overlay in the set, so it is demonstrated once rather than three times. data-hover stands in for :hover so that a file with no pointer can show it; it is a specimen affordance and not a product state, and the rule behind it is in the stylesheet with the reasons it is display: none rather than opacity: 0. The two things a CSS tooltip cannot do are the two this design most needs it to do: it cannot answer Escape, and on a phone it never appears at all, because there is no hover. The resting card in the mobile arrangement carries the indicator, which is what shows that the two arrangements put the same fact on the same line, but nothing on that card can bring up its words. A touch user gets the accessible name and nothing else, which means a sighted touch user is looking at an unexplained padlock on a name. That is the open problem in this section, it is not solved, and it is the one thing the mobile arrangement makes worse rather than better.

The panel brings its own ground rather than borrowing one, so its contrast does not depend on what is behind it. That is the same argument the scrim in the mobile arrangement makes, and it is why these two figures can be stated without a caveat about the page underneath. Its words measure 13.42:1 on that ground, and its edge, which is a control edge and is held to the same 3:1 as any other, 3.42:1. Both are checked on every run, because they are ratios of two tokens and this file hands them to the gate as data-pair. They are printed here rather than in the table above for a reason worth recording. The table sits at fourteen spaces of indent, a 67 character data-pair tag runs past the print width there, the formatter wraps the closing bracket onto the next line, and the attribute is then separated from the figure it qualifies. The gate reads line by line, so a wrapped one is a ratio nobody checks, and a ratio nobody checks is indistinguishable from a ratio nobody printed. Both were printed in that table first and both were silently unverified, which is the third time this file has been caught by its own formatter.

Every field, and whether it is shown

From PlaylistSchema, so the omissions are decisions rather than oversights. Eleven fields, six hidden. The count used to be the twelfth row here, marked as not being a field at all, and it stopped being true when the contract grew trackCount.

name not null The name, at one line, truncating.
coverUrl nullable The cover, or a note on a raised square when it is null.
syncStatus not null The state dot and word. Three values, three words.
lastSyncedAt nullable “Synced 2 days ago”, or “Never synced”.
provider not null Hidden. One value today, and a second provider would still be a detail rather than a thing a user acts on.
id not null Hidden. It is for the URL, not the reader.
ownerAccountId not null Hidden. The header is not an access control.
sourcePlaylistId not null Hidden. It is what a sync reads, not what a person reads.
createdAt not null Hidden. Last synced is the timestamp a user has a reason to want.
updatedAt not null Hidden, and this one is a trap: it moves on a rename, so it would read as a sync time and be wrong.
trackCount not null The count, drawn always, and 0 is an answer rather than a placeholder. It is a total of associations and not a promise of playability: 300 on a playlist with 290 tracks still pending is 300 and plays four songs. SyncRun.retainedCount is what one run kept, which is a different number and would be wrong here.

Six of ten fields are hidden, and that is the design doing its job. The two that earn their place are the two a user cannot guess, and updatedAt is the one most likely to be shipped by accident, because it looks exactly like lastSyncedAt and means something else entirely. trackCount was the eleventh row here and was marked as not being a field at all, which was true until the contract grew it and is the reason this table now has eleven rows and six hidden fields rather than ten and six. The one number worth being careful about on this header is still the count, because it is the one whose definition is easy to overstate.

Usage

Do show sync state here, because the header is the only place a user looks to answer “is this up to date?”. Do give every state a word as well as a colour, and a dot that survives motion stopping. Do truncate the name to one line, and do say “Never synced” rather than showing nothing or a bare date. Do design the null cover, since coverUrl is nullable and a header with a hole in it looks broken. Do show trackCount, and do draw it before the tracks have loaded, because it is a stored integer and 0 is an answer rather than a placeholder. Do not read it from SyncRun.retainedCount, which is what one run kept rather than what the playlist holds, and do not present it as a promise that the playlist will play: it counts associations, and a playlist of 300 with 290 still pending reads 300 and plays four songs. Do handle the singular. The counts that describe a run, added and removed and failed, belong to SyncRun and live in the sync summary, not here. Do not strike a failed name: the track is not deleted, and a strikethrough reads as removed. Do not explain a fault here. The sentence that says what went wrong, the reassurance about what was not lost, and the retry all live in the sync error, and this block carries the state and the time and stops. Do not colour the state with --emphasis, because amber means in progress or worth a glance and this is a fault; the state uses --tone-failed beside the word "Sync failed", so the colour never carries it alone.
